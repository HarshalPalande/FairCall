# 5-minute pitch video — shot list and script

**Razorpay AI Buildathon, Track 02 — AI Risk Manager.**
Natural runtime with everything below is ~5:30. Every number is reproducible with
`make demo` / `make backtest`. See the cutting guide at the end to land at 5:00 if you
need to.

## Before you record

```bash
make install && make demo && make backtest && make test    # warm everything
make app                                                    # Streamlit on :8501
```

Have open in tabs: terminal, `artifacts/backtest_comparison.png`, Streamlit (RDR
Optimizer + CE3.0 Qualification pages). Record at 1080p minimum — judges will be
reading terminal text.

**The one rule for this video:** every claim you make on camera should be visible
on screen as output, not asserted over a slide. That is the whole differentiator
of this submission — do not narrate it, show it.

---

## 0:00 – 0:30 — The problem, stated once

**On screen:** title card, then straight to terminal.

> "A chargeback is a customer telling their bank to reverse a payment. Most
> merchants do one of two dumb things — accept everything, or contest everything.
> Both lose money, and both ignore that a chargeback has more than one lever: you
> can refund it before it's even filed, deflect it, contest it, or send it to a
> human. We built the thing that prices all four, per dispute — scoped to a single
> reason code, Visa 13.1, 'merchandise not received.'"

Do **not** spend time on what a chargeback is beyond this. Judges know.

---

## 0:30 – 0:55 — Get the honest framing out early

**On screen:** the README's "Read this first" block.

> "One thing up front, because it changes how you should read every number that
> follows: all our data is synthetic, generated by a seeded script in this repo.
> The alternative was repurposing a fraud dataset with no dispute win/loss labels
> and quietly calling its proxy labels ground truth. We'd rather publish the exact
> data-generating process and be reproducible. This demonstrates that the system
> design works, measured against a world we disclosed in full — it is not a
> benchmarked real-world model, and we don't claim it is."

**Why this goes at 0:30 and not at 4:30:** the rubric rewards honest metrics.
Leading with the limitation converts your biggest weakness into your clearest
signal of judgment. Burying it looks like you hoped nobody would check.

---

## 0:55 – 2:30 — Four cases, two engines (the centerpiece)

**On screen:** `make demo` output, full screen, scrolled to each case in turn.
Start with the portfolio-context block it now prints first.

> "Before any case, here's the portfolio this merchant is sitting in: 200,000
> settled transactions a month, 2,600 disputes, a VAMP ratio of 1.3% against a
> 1.5% penalty threshold. 400 events of headroom. That number — the cost of ONE
> more dispute at this headroom, ₹4,576 — is what makes case four interesting.
> Hold onto it."

**Case 1 (~12s)** — 93% win probability, complete evidence, ₹1,800.

> "Two-way engine: auto-contest, easy call. Four-way engine: refund it instead —
> RDR. Even at 93% confidence, once the VAMP cost of a filed dispute is priced
> onto CONTEST and not onto a refund, refunding wins. That's not a bug, that's the
> whole thesis."

**Case 2 (~18s)** — 17% win probability, zero evidence.

> "Also easy, other direction — but look at *why* it escalates in the two-way
> engine: not the low probability, the evidence gate. It also tells the merchant
> what would help — add a tracking number, win probability goes 17% to 30%. A
> counterfactual re-score against the same model, not a generic tip. And the
> four-way engine finds a third option here: refund it. AUTO_RESOLVE doesn't need
> the evidence gate CONTEST does, because refunding is the conservative move."

**Case 3 (~35s) — slow down here. This is the ceiling.**

> "64% win probability. ₹42,000. Expected-value math says contest — EV of
> contesting is *plus* ₹11,239. A naive system with a 50% confidence threshold
> auto-contests this. Ours doesn't."
>
> "It escalates, because ₹42,000 is over our hard ceiling of ₹25,000, sitting
> deliberately *outside* the EV calculation. 99% confident, still escalates.
> There's a unit test for exactly that — and now that we have four automated
> actions instead of one, the same ceiling blocks all three of them, for the same
> reason. It's a property of the amount, not of which lever you happen to pull."

**Case 4 (~25s) — the RDR moment. This is the newest and most novel claim.**

> "69% win probability. Full evidence. Nothing blocked. The two-way engine says
> contest, EV plus ₹693. We would probably win this."
>
> "The four-way engine still refunds it — because once you price in the ₹4,576
> marginal VAMP cost that a filed dispute carries either way, refunding beats
> contesting by about ₹531. We'd probably win at representment. Refunding is
> cheaper anyway. A two-output engine literally cannot express this trade-off —
> there's no lever in it for 'give the money back on purpose.'"

**On screen for case 3's last line:** flash `tests/test_decision_engine.py`,
`test_over_ceiling_blocks_every_automated_action`.

## 2:30 – 2:55 — Two more levers, fast: CE3.0 and the RDR sweep

**On screen:** Streamlit, CE3.0 Qualification page, then RDR Optimizer page.

> "Two more pieces, quickly. CE3.0 is a deterministic rules engine — two prior
> undisputed transactions in the same 120-to-365-day window, at least two data
> elements matching across them, one of which has to be IP address or device ID —
> and a fraud dispute is blocked before it's ever filed. No model, no judgment
> call, a card-network rule coded exactly as written. It's scoped to reason code
> 10.4, not our main 13.1, because that's what CE3.0 actually applies to — we're
> not stretching it to fit."
>
> "And this is what the four-way engine looks like at portfolio scale: the RDR
> Optimizer sweeps every refund threshold and shows the trade-off, not just a
> recommendation. At this merchant's numbers, refunding under ₹4,596 takes the
> VAMP ratio from 1.3% down to 0.14% — headroom goes from 400 to over 2,700. The
> sweep is capped at the same ₹25,000 ceiling as everything else, because RDR
> moves real money automatically too."

**If short on time, cut to CE3.0's one-line result and skip the RDR sweep** — the
sweep's ₹ story is already told, more slowly, by case 4.

## 2:55 – 3:40 — The money, including the part that hurts

**On screen:** `artifacts/backtest_comparison.png`, then the `make backtest`
KEY NUMBERS block.

> "Across 3,000 held-out disputes, priced by the original two-way engine — one
> honest scope note: this backtest doesn't re-run the VAMP-aware four-way engine
> across the whole portfolio yet, because that means simulating headroom
> sequentially case by case, not scoring each one independently. The README says
> so. What you saw in case four is the real per-case effect; this is the
> contest-vs-accept economics on top of it."
>
> "Accepting everything loses ₹6.68 million. Contesting everything nets ₹773,000.
> Our system nets ₹1.08 million — about ₹312,000 better than contest-everything.
> And that's *not* better win prediction — it's that 56% of disputes get resolved
> with zero analyst time, ₹416,000 of labour contest-everything spends whether or
> not a human's judgment was needed."

**Then, deliberately:**

> "The honest cost of automating that 56%: a 14.2% false-positive rate on the
> auto-contest path. 239 cases where we said contest and the merchant lost, so
> they paid the contest fee on top of the chargeback — ₹890,000. That's a headline
> number in the repo, not buried. If your system automates decisions about money
> and you can't tell me its false-positive cost, you haven't finished."

**This is the highest-scoring 15 seconds in the video.** The rubric asks for
"honest metrics including false-positive cost" in those words. Say the number.

## 3:40 – 4:20 — Governance you can watch working

**On screen:** `make tamper-demo`, live.

> "Every decision — from either engine — is written to an append-only,
> hash-chained audit log. Everyone claims that. So watch it actually work."
>
> "Decisions logged, chain verifies. Now I edit a past entry — change an
> escalation on ₹42,000 into an auto-contest on ₹9,000, exactly what someone
> covering a bad call would do. Chain fails, and it names the line."
>
> "Then I try to bury it by appending more valid entries on top. Still fails.
> Each entry hashes the previous hash plus its own body, so editing history
> breaks every link after it. A per-entry hash with no chaining would have passed
> that first test — that's the difference, and there are tests for the deletion
> and reordering cases too."

## 4:20 – 4:55 — Where the AI is, and why it's on a leash

**On screen:** `src/evidence_draft.py`, then `tests/test_evidence_draft.py`.

> "We also prototyped the piece we'd normally be most nervous about: an LLM
> drafting the actual dispute rebuttal. An LLM asked to write a chargeback
> rebuttal will confidently assert the parcel was delivered and signed for
> whether or not that's true — in a dispute response, that's a false
> representation to a card network, not just a bad sentence."
>
> "So the model is the least-trusted component here. It never sees the raw case,
> only a fact ledger containing *only* evidence actually on file. It can't write
> the letter; it can only emit claims, each citing a fact ID. A pure-Python
> verifier rejects — doesn't repair, rejects — any draft with a fabricated
> citation, an uncited claim, or an unsupported assertion. And an approved draft
> is still just a draft. Nothing in this system submits anything anywhere."

**If short on time, cut this to 20 seconds** — keep "the model is the least
trusted component" and the rejection behaviour, drop the rest.

## Cutting guide, to land at 5:00

Natural runtime with everything above is ~5:30. Cut in this order:

1. The CE3.0 + RDR sweep segment (2:30–2:55) — saves ~25s, and case 4 already
   carries the VAMP-per-case argument on its own
2. Case 1 (0:12) — the easy case proves the least
3. The LLM draft section, trimmed to 20s per the note above — saves ~20s
4. The backtest strategy comparison, keeping only the false-positive numbers

**Never cut:** the synthetic-data disclosure (0:30), case 3 (the ceiling
override), case 4 (the RDR moment — it's the newest and most novel claim in the
submission), or the false-positive cost. Those four are the submission.

## Things not to do

- Don't read the architecture diagram aloud. It's in the README.
- Don't say "as you can see" — show the number instead.
- Don't apologise for the synthetic data a second time. Once, up front, framed as
  a decision. Repeating it reads as insecurity rather than rigour.
- Don't claim the backtest's ₹ figures include VAMP. They don't, and the video
  says so in one sentence at 2:55 — don't let a judge catch the gap you didn't
  mention.
- Don't demo the Streamlit UI just because it exists. It earns screen time for
  CE3.0 and the RDR sweep (if kept), not a tour of every page.
